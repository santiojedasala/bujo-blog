> **The short version:** you don't need complicated layouts to get value from a bullet journal. These five spreads cover 90% of your planning needs, and each one takes under 15 minutes to set up.

## Introduction

Once you have your supplies ready (a notebook, a pen and a ruler — see [our beginner's supplies guide](/posts/bullet-journal-supplies-guide/) if you're starting from zero), the next question is: what do you actually put on the page? A bullet journal can hold anything, but that freedom is also what confuses beginners. This guide walks you through the five easiest spreads to try first, in the order you'll probably want them.

## 1. The Key (Your Legend)

Before anything else, set aside one page at the front of your notebook for your **key**. It's a simple cheat-sheet of the bullets you use:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `·` | Task |
| `×` | Task completed |
| `>` | Task migrated (moved to another day) |
| `–` | Note |
| `○` | Event |
| `!` | Inspiration / important |

<div class="callout"><strong>Pro tip:</strong> you can invent your own symbols — the key just keeps them consistent so your journal stays readable weeks later.</div>

## 2. The Index

Give your bullet journal a home base: an **index** (two pages at the very front) where you write the title of each spread and its page number. That's what makes the whole system searchable — no more flipping through forty pages looking for your June tracker.

<div class="callout"><strong>Pro tip:</strong> leave a couple of blank lines between entries so you don't run out of space for edits.</div>

## 3. The Future Log (6 Months of Plans)

A **future log** is where you park things that are too far away for this month: birthdays, travel, appointments. Take one spread (two facing pages) and divide it into six sections, one per month. Anything that comes up that's further than a month away goes here instead of cluttering your daily pages.

## 4. The Monthly Log

Every month, open a fresh spread:
- **Left page** — a calendar-style grid of the month, one number per day.
- **Right page** — a task list of everything you want to get done this month (the "month top tasks").

This is the layout that keeps monthly goals visible without being overwhelming.

## 5. The Habit Tracker

Pick a simple grid: days on the rows, habits as columns, and fill in a small dot or check each day you stick with the habit. It sounds tiny, but a filled grid is incredibly motivating. Start with **3-4 habits max** (water, exercise, reading, journaling) — more than that and beginners usually quit.

<div class="callout"><strong>Pro tip:</strong> a habit tracker is also a great excuse to use your <em>washi tape</em> and <em>stencils</em> to make it pretty — just keep the functionality simple.</div>

## Bonus: The Daily Log (your everyday page)

Each day you can write a short entry with the date and just four or five lines: today's must-do tasks, one note and one event. Nothing more. It's the most flexible spread of all, and the one you'll actually use daily.

## What to Skip at First

- **Fancy collections** (reading lists, movie logs) — add them later, not now.
- **Complex art** — doodles are optional; function first.
- **Ten trackers in month one** — you'll overwhelm yourself.

## Conclusion

A bullet journal starts with just three essentials on the page: a key, an index, and some kind of core weekly/monthly rhythm. Start with the five spreads above, keep them simple, and let your system grow with you. The perfect journal is not the prettiest one — it's the one that keeps you organized for more than a week.
